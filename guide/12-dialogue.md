# Dialogue: Make Voices Different
*8 min read*

**The picture:** Cover the names in a group chat. Can you still tell who is talking? Real people have a style: emojis, short replies, full sentences. Your characters need one too.

**Dialogue** is the words your characters say out loud, inside quotation marks. Good dialogue does three jobs at once: it sounds like a real person, it shows who they are, and it pushes the story forward. Bad dialogue sounds like a robot reading a script.

## The rules

- **Every line does a job:** reveals character, moves the plot, or raises tension. Cut small talk that does none of them.
- **Give each character a voice.** Think about word choice, sentence length, favorite phrases, what they never say.
- **Use subtext.** (**Subtext** is the meaning under the words, the thing a character feels but does not say.) People rarely say what they mean. "Fine." can mean "I'm furious." Let the reader read between the lines.
- **Let characters want different things in the same conversation.** That is where sparks fly.
- **Keep it short.** Cut greetings and filler. Enter late, leave early.
- **Read it aloud.** If you stumble, readers will too.
- **Mix in action.** A gesture or object gives a talking scene something to see.
- **Do not use dialogue to dump info** ("As you know, Bob, our planet has two moons...").

## Tags and beats

A **tag** is the little label that says who spoke: "she said." A **beat** is a bit of action instead of a tag: *Mia slammed her locker.* Beats do double duty because they show who is talking and add a picture.

"Said" is nearly invisible, which is good. Readers skim right past it. Save fancy tags (whispered, snapped, hissed) for when they matter. And skip the adverb pileup: "he said angrily" is weaker than a line that already sounds angry.

## How to build a voice

Pick three knobs for each character:

| Knob | Example A | Example B |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| Sentence length | Short. Blunt. | Long, winding, full of "and then" |
| Favorite words | "Obviously" | "Kinda," "like" |
| What they avoid | Never says sorry | Never says no |

A character who always asks questions sounds different from one who gives orders. A character who jokes when scared sounds different from one who goes silent.

## Worked example 1: the same line, three voices

The situation: someone bumps into a person carrying a cake.

> **Blunt kid:** "You wrecked it."
> **Chatty kid:** "Oh no, oh no, okay, that was totally my fault, I wasn't looking, are you okay? Is the cake okay? It's not okay, is it?"
> **Polite kid:** "I believe we may have a small problem with the dessert."

Same event, three different people. If you took out the names, you could still guess who is who.

## Worked example 2: before and after (info dump)

**Before**

> "Hello, Tara," said Mr. Lin. "As you know, I am your science teacher and I have taught at this school for twenty years. You are worried about the science fair, which is on Friday."
> "Yes, Mr. Lin. I am worried because my volcano project broke and I have no time."

**Why it fails:** Nobody talks like this. Each person tells the other things they both already know, purely for the reader's benefit. There is no tension and no personality.

**After**

> Mr. Lin looked at the ruined volcano. Baking soda coated the table like snow.
> "Friday," he said.
> "I know."
> "That's three days."
> "I *know*." Tara picked at the paper-mache. "Could the project be a failed volcano? Like, a study of why they fail?"
> He almost smiled. "Now that is a project."

**What changed:** The facts (volcano, Friday, trouble) come out through action and short lines. Tara has a voice (a little defensive, a little clever). Mr. Lin shows care without saying it. That is subtext.

## Worked example 3: before and after (everyone sounds the same)

**Before**

> "I am upset that you took my sweater," said Noor.
> "I am sorry that I took your sweater," said Dev. "I will return it tomorrow."
> "I would appreciate that," said Noor.

**Why it fails:** Both speak in the same careful, perfect sentences. Nobody wants anything. It reads like a form letter.

**After**

> "Is that my sweater?" Noor said.
> Dev pulled his sleeves over his hands. "It's a sweater."
> "It has my name on the tag."
> "Lots of people are named Noor."
> "Dev."
> "Okay, okay. It was cold."

**What changed:** Dev dodges (he wants to avoid blame). Noor corners him (she wants the truth). Short lines, a bit of humor, and a beat (the sleeves) show guilt without the word "guilt."

## Worked example 4: subtext

**Weak (on the nose)**

> "I'm sad that you are moving away," said Lena. "I will miss you very much."

**Stronger**

> Lena kept stacking boxes. "Do they have good pizza where you're going?"
> "I don't know."
> "You should find out. For research."
> "Research," Sam said.
> Neither of them looked up.

**Why it works:** Lena never says she is sad. She talks about pizza, and we feel it anyway. When emotions are big, small words often hit harder.

## Worked example 5: a scene where people want different things

**Weak version**

> "Can I borrow your notes?" asked Ben.
> "Sure," said Ava. "Here you go."
> "Thanks," said Ben.

**Why it fails:** Everyone agrees, so there is no spark. It is also a conversation we have no reason to read.

**Stronger version**

> "Can I borrow your notes?" Ben asked.
> Ava didn't look up. "Which ones?"
> "All of them."
> "All of them." She flipped a page. "Funny. You didn't ask when I was sick last week."
> "I was busy."
> "Busy." She slid the notebook across the desk, then kept two fingers on it. "Bring it back by lunch. With a snack."

**What changed:** Ben wants notes. Ava wants some respect (and a snack). The two goals rub, and we learn about their history in a few lines. Notice the repeated words, which is how people often echo and push back.

## Workshop: five quick fixes for flat dialogue

1. **Cut the first and last line.** Most conversations start too early and end too late.
2. **Swap a question for a statement.** "Are you okay?" becomes "You look terrible."
3. **Add an object.** Give someone a spoon, a phone, a leash. Let them use it.
4. **Give one character a secret.** Even a small one changes how they answer.
5. **Interrupt someone.** Real talk gets cut off.

Try each one on a single page of your own dialogue and keep the version you like best.

## Common mistakes

- **Everyone sounds like you.** Give each character their own rhythm and words.
- **Talking heads.** Pages of back-and-forth with no setting or action. Add a gesture or object.
- **As-you-know speeches.** Characters explain what both already know.
- **Perfect grammar all the time.** Real people interrupt, trail off, and say "um." (Use a little, not a lot.)
- **Too many fancy tags.** "He exclaimed, she retorted, they chortled." Use "said."
- **Greetings and goodbyes.** Skip "Hi, how are you?" unless it matters. Enter late, leave early.
- **Saying the feeling.** "I'm angry!" is weaker than slamming a door while saying "Great."
- **Heavy accents spelled out.** Hard to read and can feel unkind. Show voice through word choice and rhythm instead.

## Punctuation cheat sheet

- A new speaker means a **new paragraph**.
- Use a dash (—) for an interrupted line, and an ellipsis (...) for a trailing-off line.
- Commas go inside the quotation marks: "Fine," she said.

## Percy Jackson check

Percy's narration is sarcastic and funny. Annabeth is precise and strategic. Grover is nervous and loyal. You can tell who is speaking without a name. The jokes also hide fear, which is subtext at work: Percy cracks wise when things are scary, so the humor tells us how he copes.

Notice, too, how the characters often want different things in the same conversation: one wants a plan, one wants to run, one wants to protect a friend. That friction keeps the talk lively.

Our own imitation (not from the book): *"We have a plan," said the strategist. "Is it a good plan?" said the joker. "It has a plan-shaped hole in it."* Two voices, two attitudes, in three short lines.

## Mini-quiz

**1. What is subtext?**
The meaning under the words: what a character feels but does not say.

**2. Which tag is best most of the time: "said," "exclaimed," or "declared"?**
"Said." It is almost invisible.

**3. Two characters both say, "I understand your concern." What is the problem?**
They sound the same. Give each a different rhythm, vocabulary, and attitude.

## Try it (20 min)

Write a 10-line argument between two characters about something small (the last slice of pizza). No name tags. Ask a friend who is who.

## Level up

**The one-word challenge.** Write a conversation where one character can only answer in short phrases (three words or fewer) and the other talks a lot. Make the short-answer character the one with the secret. Then rewrite it so the roles flip. Notice how the power in the scene shifts with the rhythm.

Bonus: write the same scene as a text-message thread. What does each person's texting style say about them?
