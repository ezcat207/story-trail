# The Opening: Start Where Things Change
*8 min read*

**The picture:** Walking into a party, you decide in about ten seconds whether to stay. Readers do the same with your first page. Start at the doorway of the loud room, not in the parking lot.

Your opening has a small job and a big job. The small job: get the reader to read page two. The big job: tell them what kind of story this is and who to care about. You do not need to explain the whole world. You just need to open the door.

## The rules

- **Start at the moment normal breaks.** Not at birth, not at "a regular Tuesday," not with the hero waking up and brushing their teeth.
- **Raise a question in the first 200 words or so.** Who is that? Why is she running? What is in the box? A question the reader wants answered is a hook (a pull that keeps people reading).
- **Show a hint of normal life through action,** not a lecture. One telling detail beats a paragraph of backstory. (**Backstory** is what happened before the story started.)
- **Make it clear whose story this is.** Readers need someone to hold onto.
- **Promise something.** The opening tells the reader what kind of ride this is: funny, scary, strange.
- **Ground the reader.** Give just enough: who, where, and a hint of when. Confused is good. Lost is not.
- **Use a voice.** A distinct way of talking makes a page feel alive even before the plot gets going.

## Where to cut in

Write your opening, then ask: could I cut the first page and lose nothing? If yes, cut it. Many writers find their real first line hiding on page two or three.

A handy trick: find the first sentence where something *happens or changes*. Start there, and slip any needed background into later lines.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Starting in the parking lot

> It was a sunny Saturday morning in the town of Millbrook. Birds sang in the trees. Dara woke up, stretched, and went downstairs to eat cereal. Her mom was reading the newspaper. Dara had a soccer game later that day, and she was a little nervous about it.

**Why it fails:** Nothing is wrong. Nothing is strange. The reader learns the weather, the cereal, and the mom's hobby. Nothing pulls them forward. "A little nervous" is too vague to feel.

**Stronger version:**

> The cereal box on the counter had Dara's name on it, which was weird, because Dara had never bought cereal in her life. Inside was a note: *Do not play in today's game.* She glanced at her mom, who was reading the paper and had not looked up once.

**Why it works:** We still see breakfast, a mom, and an ordinary kitchen. But now the ordinary has a crack in it. A question appears right away: who wrote the note, and why?

### Example 2: The info dump

> Long ago, the five kingdoms of Aerlon were ruled by the Sun Council, who drew their power from the seven crystals. Each crystal had a different color and a different purpose. The Red Crystal powered fire, the Blue Crystal powered water, and so on. Kael was a farm boy who lived in the third kingdom.

**Why it fails:** This is a history lesson. A reader has to remember five kingdoms, seven crystals, and a council before meeting a single person. Nobody is in trouble yet.

**Stronger version:**

> Kael was hauling water when the well started glowing. Not a little glow, either. Blue light climbed out of the bucket and up his arm. The last person in his village who had seen light like that had been taken away by soldiers.

**Why it works:** The world still shows up (soldiers, magic, a village). But it arrives through a person and an event, and we only get what we need to feel the danger. The crystals can wait until chapter three.

### Example 3: Voice and promise

> Hello. My name is Theo, and I have to warn you: this is the story of how I got stuck in a vending machine. If you are the kind of person who likes things to make sense, you should put this book down now.

**Why it works:** In three sentences, we learn the narrator's voice (funny, a little cranky), the promise (a weird, comic mess), and a question (how did he get *in* there?). Not every book needs a voice this loud. But every opening needs to promise *something*.

### Example 4: Too many names

> Captain Voss, Lieutenant Marrow, Dr. Penhallow, young Tilly, old Barnaby, and the twins, Rex and Rue, all stood on the deck of the *Silver Gull* as it left Port Alder.

**Why it fails:** That is seven names and two places in one sentence. The reader does not know who to follow, so they follow no one.

**Stronger version:**

> Tilly gripped the rail of the *Silver Gull* and tried not to look at the Captain, who was looking right at her. Everyone else on deck had gone quiet. She was the only one who knew what was in the crate.

**Why it works:** One hero, one ship, one tense secret. The others can be named when they matter, one at a time.

## The first-page checklist

Before you move on, test your first page with these quick questions:

1. Do I know **who** this is about?
2. Do I know roughly **where** we are?
3. Is something **wrong, odd, or changing**?
4. Is there a **question** I want answered?
5. Can I **hear a voice**, even a quiet one?

If you can say yes to at least four, you are in good shape. If not, pick the weakest one and fix it first.

## Quick fixes for a flat first line

- Replace a general statement with a specific object. Not "It was a strange day," but "The bus driver was wearing two left shoes."
- Move a line of dialogue up. People talking is faster than people thinking.
- Cut the first sentence and see if the second one works better. It often does.

## A note on perfection

Your opening is the part you will rewrite the most. That is normal. Many writers draft the first chapter last, because only then do they know what the story actually promises. If you are stuck, skip ahead and come back. A rough opening that lets you finish a draft beats a perfect one that keeps you frozen.

## Common mistakes

1. **A weather report or long description before anything happens.** One sharp detail does the job of five.
2. **An info dump about the world, history, or magic rules.** Slip it in later, a little at a time.
3. **Too many names in the first page.** Three characters, maximum. Four, if you are brave.
4. **Starting too early,** with ten pages before the real trouble.
5. **A fake-out** ("it was all a dream") that cheats the reader.
6. **Starting with a mirror.** Hero looks in the mirror to describe themselves. It is a cliche for a reason.
7. **Action with no person in it.** A car chase is boring if we do not know who is in the car or why they care.
8. **Hiding everything to be mysterious.** A question is good. Total confusion is not.

## Percy Jackson check

Percy's story begins with a school trip going wrong, and with the narrator warning the reader: if you think you might be a demigod, close this book. It sets voice, humor, and danger in a few lines. Then a teacher turns into a monster. Normal life breaks quickly.

What to steal: the book makes a *promise* in its first lines, a funny but dangerous tone, and keeps it. You can see the voice before you see the plot.

*Our own imitation line, not from the book:* "If your lunch lady ever asks to see your hands, and she says it in a very low voice, do not hand over your pudding. Run."

## Mini-quiz

1. Why is "a regular Tuesday" a risky way to begin?
2. What is a hook?
3. You have a cool magic system with seven rules. Where should the rules go in your opening?

**Answers**

1. Because nothing has changed yet. Without a crack in normal life, readers have no reason to worry or wonder.
2. Something in the writing, usually a question or a surprise, that makes the reader want to keep going.
3. Mostly not in the opening. Show one rule in action through a character, and save the rest for later when the reader needs it.

## Try it (20 min)

Take your first paragraph. Move the story's first real problem into the first five sentences. Read it aloud. If you stumble or get bored, mark that spot and rewrite it.

## Level up

Write the same opening three ways:

1. **Action first:** start with the hero in the middle of trouble.
2. **Voice first:** start with the narrator talking directly to the reader.
3. **Strange detail first:** start with one odd object or fact.

Pick your favorite and explain in one sentence why it grabs you the most. Ask a friend to choose which one they would keep reading. You might be surprised.
