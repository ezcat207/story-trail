# Hooks: Make Them Turn the Page
*8 min read*

**The picture:** A hook is a fish hook. A small, sharp thing that catches attention and does not let go.

Readers have a lot of choices: videos, games, other books. A hook says, "Stay. Something is about to happen." It shows up in three places: your first lines, the end of each chapter, and your teaser (the short description that sells the story).

## Opening hooks (first lines or paragraph)

- **A strange fact:** "The day my brother disappeared, the clocks kept running."
- **A voice:** a narrator with attitude.
- **A question:** something the reader needs answered.
- **A promise:** "This is the story of how I got grounded by a god."
- **A character in motion:** someone already doing something, not waking up and brushing teeth.

A good opening hook usually has two parts: something **unusual** and something **unanswered**. Unusual catches the eye. Unanswered keeps it.

## Worked example 1: the opening line

**Weak version**

> It was a normal Tuesday, and Ravi woke up and went to school like always.

**Why it fails:** "Normal" tells us nothing will happen. No surprise, no question, no voice.

**Stronger versions**

> On Tuesday, Ravi found out his school had been dead for nine years.
> Ravi's only rule was "never open the locker at the end of the hall." On Tuesday he forgot the rule.
> Nobody warned Ravi that the new kid would be a robot. Nobody warned the robot about Ravi, either.

**What changed:** Each one shows something odd (dead school, forbidden locker, robot) and leaves a question (How? What's inside? What happens next?). Notice these did not need to explode with action. A tiny crack in normal life is enough.

## Chapter-end hooks

- End on a **cliffhanger**: a choice, a reveal, danger. (A **cliffhanger** is an ending that stops at a tense moment, before we know the outcome.)
- End on a **new question**.
- End on a **small surprise** that changes how we read what came before.
- Not every chapter needs a big one. Vary them, or readers feel tricked.

## Worked example 2: the chapter ending

**Weak version**

> Mia finished her homework and went to bed. She was tired. The end of the day came.

**Why it fails:** It closes the door softly. Nothing pulls the reader on. This reads like a nap.

**Stronger versions**

> Mia turned off the lamp. That was when her phone lit up with a text from her own number: *Don't answer the door.*

> The test was over, and Mia had passed. That was the problem. She had not studied.

> Mia put the key in the lock. It turned. But she had never told anyone she had the key.

**What changed:** Each ending plants a small fire. A strange message, a secret, a violated rule. The reader thinks, "I'll just read one more page."

**Tip:** A good chapter-end hook is usually *one new problem* or *one new question*, not both a giant explosion and five questions. Keep it clean.

## Types of chapter-end hooks, side by side

| Type | Feels like | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Danger | "Uh-oh." | The bridge started to crack. |
| Reveal | "Wait, what?" | The map was in her mother's handwriting. |
| Choice | "What will they pick?" | Door A saves the dog. Door B saves the team. |
| Question | "Hmm..." | Why had the teacher lied? |
| Reversal | "I did not see that." | The friend was the thief all along. |

## The Z-shape teaser (for the blurb or first page)

A **teaser** (or blurb) is a short pitch that makes someone want to open your story. Think of your eyes tracing a Z: top-left to top-right (the premise), diagonal down (the problem), bottom-left to bottom-right (the stakes and the question). Give a teaser that follows that path: who, what trouble, what is at risk.

## Worked example 3: a teaser

**Weak version**

> This is a story about a girl named Zoe who has an adventure and learns a lot about herself. It is exciting and has many twists.

**Why it fails:** "Adventure," "learns a lot," and "twists" are labels, not content. We learn nothing specific.

**Stronger version (Z-shape)**

> *Top line (premise):* Twelve-year-old Zoe can hear every secret said inside her school's walls.
> *Diagonal (problem):* When she overhears the principal planning to close the school, she is the only one who knows.
> *Bottom line (stakes and question):* She has one week to prove it, but her proof is a secret she can never admit she overheard. Can she save the school without losing her friends' trust?

**What changed:** Specific hero, specific power, specific problem, a clock (one week), and a question at the end.

## Worked example 4: fixing a fake hook

**Weak version**

> You will not believe what happened next! It was the most shocking thing ever. Chapter 4 will tell you.

**Why it fails:** It shouts that something is exciting without showing a single detail. It is the story version of "click here."

**Stronger version**

> The lights went out. In the dark, Tomas heard the sound of someone else breathing in the room, and it was not Grandpa.

**What changed:** We get a real image and a concrete scary fact. The reader supplies the shock themselves.

## A five-minute hook workout

Try this warm-up whenever you feel stuck:

1. Write the boring version of your opening ("Sam walked to school").
2. Ask: what is the **one odd detail** I could add? ("Sam walked to school, carrying a cage that wasn't his.")
3. Ask: what question does that raise? ("Whose cage? What is in it?")
4. Put the odd detail first.

It works almost every time because strange details create questions, and questions pull readers forward.

## Where hooks hide inside a chapter

Hooks are not only for openings and endings. Sprinkle small ones through the middle too:

- **A promise line:** "If she had known what was in the box, she would have left it alone."
- **A ticking detail:** a clock, a countdown, a battery at 3 percent.
- **An unanswered question:** a character starts to say something, then stops.

These tiny tugs keep readers moving through the slow parts.

## Rules

- **Pay off what you hook.** A hook with no payoff is clickbait, and readers remember. (**Payoff** is the answer or result you promised.)
- **Do not hook with lies.** Surprise is good. Cheating is not. A "dream" ending that erases the whole chapter feels like a cheat.
- **Match the tone.** A funny hook that leads into a grim story confuses readers.
- **Keep it short.** A hook is a sting, not a speech.
- **Vary the rhythm.** Mix loud cliffhangers with quieter, curious endings.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with weather or a wake-up.** Unless the weather is trying to kill someone.
- **Starting with a long backstory.** Let the reader meet trouble first, history later.
- **The fake-out cliffhanger.** "She opened the door and... it was just the cat." Once is funny. Often is annoying.
- **Hooks that do not fit the story.** A shark attack in a story about a spelling bee.
- **Every chapter ending the same way.** Mix danger, reveal, and question.
- **Too much too soon.** If your first line is the end of the world, what do you do for the rest?
- **Teasers that spoil the ending.** Show the problem, not the solution.

## Percy Jackson check

Chapter titles are tiny hooks on their own, and many chapters end with a new threat or a revealed secret. The first page tells you flat out that this will be a bumpy ride: the narrator warns you that being a half-blood is not a fun thing, and that you might want to put the book down. That is a **voice** hook and a **promise** hook in one move.

Our own imitation (not from the book): *"If you're reading this, you've probably already noticed the weird things happening in your life. Close the book now. Seriously."* The twist is that telling a reader to leave makes them stay.

## Mini-quiz

**1. What two parts does a good opening hook usually have?**
Something unusual and something unanswered.

**2. Why is a hook with no payoff a problem?**
Readers feel tricked, like they clicked on clickbait, and they stop trusting you.

**3. In the Z-shape teaser, what goes on the bottom line?**
The stakes (what could be lost) and the big question.

## Try it (20 min)

Write five different first lines for your story. Read them to someone. Ask which one made them want line two.

## Level up

**Reverse-engineer a hook.** Pick three books on your shelf. Copy only the first line and the last line of chapter one of each (do not copy more than that, and do not publish them). Label each: strange fact, voice, question, promise, cliffhanger, reveal. Then write one of your own chapter endings using a type you have not tried yet.

Bonus: write a teaser for your story in exactly three sentences, one for each part of the Z.
