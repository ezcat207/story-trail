# The Ending: A Chest with Two Locks
*8 min read*

**The picture:** A treasure chest with two locks needs two keys. A good ending needs two things to click.

You have done the hard work. The big fight is over. Now readers want one more thing: to feel that it was worth it. The ending is the last taste in their mouth, and it decides how they remember the whole meal.

## The two locks

**Lock 1: the action ending.** The outside problem is solved or settled. The war is stopped, the race is won, the secret is out.

**Lock 2: the meaning ending.** The reader sees how the hero changed, or what the story was really about. A look, a quiet moment, a small action can say it.

If you only open Lock 1, the story feels like a checklist that got finished. If you only open Lock 2, the reader asks, "Wait, what happened to the problem?" You need both.

## The rules

- **Tie the loose threads.** If you introduced something, finish it or deliberately leave it open.
- **Do not explain the moral out loud.** Show the hero doing something differently than they did at the start.
- **Do not drag on.** After the climax, a few paragraphs to a couple of pages. Not ten.
- **Echo the opening.** Return to an image from the beginning, but changed. This gives readers a satisfying circle.
- **Last image matters.** Readers remember the beginning and the end most. Make the last line count.
- **Sequel hooks are okay,** but the story in front of you must finish first.
- **Pay the price.** If the climax cost something, the ending should show that cost, even in a small way.
- **Match the promise.** A funny book can end on a joke. A scary book can end with a shiver. Do not switch genres in the last page.

## Kinds of endings

| Type | What it feels like | Watch out for |
|------|--------------------|---------------|
| Happy | The hero wins and gets what they wanted | Too tidy, no cost |
| Bittersweet | The hero wins, but loses something | Too sad for the story's tone |
| Open | Some questions are left for the reader | Feeling unfinished |
| Twist | The last page flips what we thought | A trick with no clues earlier |

None of these is better than the others. Pick the one that fits your story's promise.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: The lecture ending

> Jada brought the lost puppy home and her parents let her keep it. She learned that kindness always matters and that if you work hard, good things will happen. The end.

**Why it fails:** The last two sentences tell us the moral. Readers hate being told what to think. Also, nothing shows that Jada is different from the girl at the start.

**Stronger version:**

> That night, Jada put a bowl of water by the back door, the same door she used to slam. When the puppy yipped, she did not pretend she hadn't heard. She got out of bed, wrapped it in the blanket she'd always said she hated sharing, and fell asleep on the kitchen floor.

**Why it works:** No moral is stated. But we *see* it: the girl who once slammed doors and guarded her stuff now gives up her bed and her blanket.

### Example 2: The circle

**Opening line of the story:** "Every morning, Owen counted the steps to the bus stop. Forty-two. He never skipped any."

**Weak ending:** "Owen finally felt better about things and went to school."

**Stronger ending:** "The next morning, Owen walked to the bus stop without counting. He did not even notice, until the bus was already there."

**Why it works:** The image from the opening (counting steps) returns changed. The reader gets a small thrill of recognition. It does the work of an explanation without any explanation.

### Example 3: Drags on too long

> The monster was defeated. Everyone cheered. Then the village held a parade. After that, the mayor gave a speech. Then there was a feast, and later everyone went home and went to bed. The next day, they all woke up, ate breakfast, and talked about the monster again.

**Why it fails:** The excitement ended two pages ago. The reader is waiting for a point that already happened.

**Stronger version:**

> The village cheered until dark. Pip didn't. He walked home alone, and at the edge of the field he stopped. The monster's footprints were still pressed into the mud, huge and silly now, like a giant's slippers. He knelt and set his own small boot inside one. It didn't come close to filling it. He smiled.

**Why it works:** One image, one quiet beat, and the story closes. The footprint shows both the size of what he did and how much he has grown.

### Example 4: Both locks in a tiny story

Imagine a story about a boy, Marco, who has been too scared to speak in class, and who is entering the school talent show with a magic trick.

> **Lock 1 (action):** The trick works. The rabbit appears. The crowd claps.
>
> **Lock 2 (meaning):** After the show, a classmate asks him a question. Marco answers out loud, in a normal voice, before he realizes he has done it.

If you stop at the applause, readers cheer and forget. If you add the small answer, they smile and remember. The talent show was the plot. The answer was the point.

## A quick ending test

Read your last page and ask:

1. **Did the main problem get settled?** (Lock 1)
2. **Can I see how the hero is different?** (Lock 2)
3. **Is there any thread I forgot?**
4. **Does the last line leave an image, not a lecture?**
5. **Would a reader feel like closing the book with a small sigh?**

That sigh is the sound of a lock clicking.

## When you are stuck on an ending

Try writing the last scene from the opposite direction. Start with the final image you want the reader to have, then work backward: what had to happen right before that? What choice made it possible? Many writers find that the ending clicks into place once they know the last picture. If you still cannot see it, look at your first page. Your ending is often a changed version of your beginning, waiting for you to notice it.

## Common mistakes

1. **Stating the moral.** "And so Jada learned..." Cut it. Show it.
2. **Dragging on.** Five more scenes after the climax.
3. **Ending too fast.** "And then everything was fine." Give the reader a moment to feel it.
4. **Forgotten threads.** The missing sibling or lost key that never comes back.
5. **A new problem at the last second.** Save it for a sequel, or fold it into the story earlier.
6. **A "just kidding" ending.** It was a dream, a simulation, or a prank. It cheats the work the reader did.
7. **No echo.** The opening and ending feel like they belong to different stories.
8. **Weak last line.** The final sentence is an explanation or a shrug. Aim for an image or action.

## Percy Jackson check

The quest ends with the bolt returned and the war avoided. Then there is a quieter beat about family and where Percy belongs now. The outside problem is solved, the inside one starts to heal, and a hint at a bigger future is left for the next book.

Look at how the two locks work: the bolt (Lock 1) is the plot, and the question of family and belonging (Lock 2) is the heart. The ending also leaves a door open without leaving the main story unfinished.

*Our own imitation line, not from the book:* "He set his bag on the empty bunk, and for the first time, the room felt like it was waiting for him instead of the other way around."

## Mini-quiz

1. What are the two locks of an ending?
2. Why should you avoid saying the moral out loud?
3. What does it mean to "echo the opening"?

**Answers**

1. The action ending (the outside problem is settled) and the meaning ending (the reader sees how the hero changed).
2. Readers trust what they see more than what they are told, and a stated moral feels like a lecture.
3. Bring back an image, action, or line from the beginning, but changed to show growth.

## Try it (20 min)

Reread your first page. Write a last paragraph that echoes it with one thing changed. Then check both locks: what is the action that ends, and what is the change in your hero that we can see?

## Level up

Write three different last lines for your story: one funny, one quiet, and one that points to the future. Read all three aloud. Which one feels like the *click* of the second lock? Then try this experiment: delete the last paragraph of your draft and read from the previous one. Sometimes the best ending is one paragraph earlier than you think.
