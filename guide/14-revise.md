# Revise and Score: Be Your Own Coach
*8 min read*

**The picture:** An athlete watches game film. Not to feel bad, but to find what to train. Revision is your film session.

First drafts are supposed to be messy. Professional writers say so all the time. A first draft is clay on the table; revision is where you shape it. If your draft feels bad, congratulations: you have a draft.

**Revision** means "seeing again." It is not just fixing typos. It is asking, "Does this story do what I want it to do?"

## The process

1. **Finish the draft first.** Do not polish page one forever.
2. **Rest.** A day or two away helps you see it fresh.
3. **Read aloud.** Where did you trip? Get bored? Get confused?
4. **Score it** with the rubric on the Score My Story tab. (A **rubric** is a checklist with levels that tells you how well each part works.)
5. **Fix the top one to three problems only.** Big things first (structure, stakes, climax), small things last (word choice, commas).
6. **Re-score.** Did the number go up for the right reasons?
7. **Share with a real person** and ask one specific question, like "Where did you get lost?"

## Big to small: the revision ladder

Fix things in this order. Polishing a sentence you will later delete is wasted time.

| Level | Ask | Example fix |
|-------|-----|-------------|
| 1. Story | Is the goal clear? Are the stakes real? Does the climax answer the problem? | Add a ticking clock |
| 2. Scenes | Does each scene have a want, a wall, and a change? | Cut a scene where nothing happens |
| 3. Paragraphs | Is the order clear? Any confusing jumps? | Move the reveal earlier |
| 4. Sentences | Are they clear, varied, and strong? | Replace a vague verb |
| 5. Words and commas | Spelling, punctuation, repeated words | Run a final check |

## Worked example 1: fixing a story-level problem

**Draft problem:** "My story has a hero who wants to win the school robot contest. She builds a robot, enters the contest, and wins. The end."

**Why it fails:** Nothing goes wrong. The hero wins without a fight. The score for "conflict" and "stakes" would be low.

**Revision plan (top three fixes only)**
1. Add a wall: her partner quits a week before the contest.
2. Add stakes: if she loses, the robotics club closes.
3. Make the climax cost something: her robot breaks mid-match and she must choose between fixing it and helping the rival whose robot caught fire.

**Result:** Same story seed, now with a goal, a wall, a clock, and a choice. Note that we did not touch word choice at all. Big stuff first.

## Worked example 2: fixing a sentence-level problem

**Weak version**

> The dog was very big and it ran really fast and it was very scary and then it jumped over the fence.

**Why it fails:** Five "and"s in a row, vague words ("very," "really"), and no picture.

**Stronger version**

> The dog was the size of a small couch. It hit the fence at a full sprint, and the whole thing rattled, and then it was over, paws scrabbling, eyes on me.

**What changed:** A specific comparison, a stronger verb, a clear order of events, and a person's point of view ("eyes on me") that adds fear.

Notice we only changed this after the big problems were fixed.

## Worked example 3: a feedback conversation

**Poor question to a friend:** "Did you like it?"
Answer: "Yeah, it was good." (Nice, but useless.)

**Better questions:**
- "Where did you get bored?"
- "Which character did you care about most?"
- "What did you think would happen at the end?"
- "Was there a place you had to reread?"

**Why it works:** Specific questions get specific answers. You are looking for the **spot** where the reader left your story, not a compliment.

## Worked example 4: a revision plan on paper

Suppose you scored your story like this on a 1 to 5 scale:

| Area | Score | Note |
|------|-------|------|
| Hook | 4 | Strong first line |
| Hero | 3 | We know what she wants, not why |
| Conflict | 2 | The villain just shows up |
| Middle | 2 | Three scenes feel the same |
| Climax | 3 | Fine, but too easy |
| Ending | 4 | Lands well |

**Weak plan:** "Fix everything."

**Why it fails:** Too big. You will get tired and fix nothing.

**Stronger plan (top three):**
1. Give the villain a reason (conflict).
2. Merge two of the three similar middle scenes and make the third harder (middle).
3. Make the climax cost the hero something (climax).

Leave the hook and ending alone. They are working. Re-score after one pass and see if the numbers moved.

## A read-aloud checklist

When you read your story out loud, put a mark in the margin whenever you:
- **Trip** over a sentence (too long or clumsy).
- **Yawn** (the scene is slow).
- **Squint** (something is confusing).
- **Smile or gasp** (keep it!).

Then fix the trips and squints first, and tighten the yawns.

## How to take feedback

- **Don't defend. Listen first.** Write the note down, even if it stings.
- **Look for patterns.** If one person is confused, maybe it's them. If three people trip in the same spot, it's the page.
- **Fix the problem, not necessarily their solution.** A reader may be right that something is wrong but wrong about how to fix it. You know your story best.
- **Thank them.** Honest readers are rare.

## Using AI the right way

- Paste your story into an AI with the rubric prompt. It will give feedback and a score, and it should not rewrite your story.
- Disagree sometimes. If the feedback is wrong, say why. That is you being the author.
- Tell readers if AI helped you with feedback.
- Ask it questions like, "Where is the weakest scene and why?" instead of "Make this better."
- Remember: a score is a coach's note, not a verdict on you.

## What scores mean

- 60 to 75: a solid first draft.
- 76 to 85: strong and clear.
- 86+: rare. Be proud.

If your number is lower than you hoped, look at it as a map: it shows where the next hour of work should go.

## Common mistakes

- **Polishing too early.** Making chapter one perfect before the story exists.
- **Fixing everything at once.** Pick the top one to three problems, then re-score.
- **Fixing small stuff first.** Commas are not the problem if the climax is missing.
- **Deleting your voice.** Revision should make your story more yours, not more like everyone else's.
- **Only asking people who love you.** Kind friends are great, but include at least one honest reader.
- **Ignoring all feedback or accepting all feedback.** Think about each note.
- **Never finishing.** At some point, say "done enough," and write the next story.
- **Letting a score feel like a grade on you.** It grades the draft, not the writer.

## Percy Jackson check

Even a famous book went through drafts. Authors rewrite, cut scenes, and sharpen jokes before a book reaches shelves. Notice the places in the book that made you laugh, gasp, or care. Those are the parts to study: ask what the author did in that scene that made you feel it. Was it a surprise, a sharp line of dialogue, a risk to a character you love?

You can also test your own draft the same way: mark where *you* laughed, gasped, or cared. If you marked nothing, that is your revision list.

## Mini-quiz

**1. Which should you fix first: a missing climax or a comma?**
The missing climax. Big things first.

**2. Why rest a day or two before revising?**
You see the draft with fresh eyes and spot problems you were too close to notice.

**3. What is a better question for a reader than "Did you like it?"**
A specific one, such as "Where did you get lost?" or "Where were you bored?"

## Try it (20 min)

Score your story yourself from 1 to 5 for each of the eight rubric areas before you ask the AI. Compare. Where do you disagree?

## Level up

**The reverse outline.** After your draft is done, write one line for each scene: *who wants what, what blocks them, what changes.* Lay the lines out in order.

Then circle any scene where one of the three is missing. For each circled scene, do one of three things: fix it, merge it with another scene, or cut it. Re-read your story. Did it get faster? Did the middle get stronger?

Bonus: read the story aloud into your phone's voice recorder and listen back with your eyes closed. Note every spot where you get restless.
