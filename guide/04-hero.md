# Hero, Want, and Cost: The Engine
*9 min read*

**The picture:** A car needs fuel and a destination. Your hero's **want** is the destination. The **cost of failing** is the fuel, because it is the reason the hero keeps driving when the road gets bad. A car with a destination and no fuel sits in the driveway. A car with fuel and no destination just goes in circles. You need both.

When readers say a character "feels real," they usually mean this: the character wants something badly, and we believe it.

## The rules

- **A want must be specific.** "Be happy" is a wish. "Win the regional robotics trophy so Dad notices me" is a want. A good test: could you film it? You can film someone building a robot. You cannot film "happiness."
- **Give a reason they cannot walk away.** If the hero can quit with no consequences, the reader can too. Ask: what happens if they just go home?
- **Wants and needs are different.** The **want** is the thing they chase. The **need** is what they actually have to learn. Great stories let them chase one and find the other. A kid wants to win the trophy (want) but needs to learn to ask for help (need).
- **Make the hero act.** Heroes who just react to things happening are passengers. Let them make choices, even wrong ones. Wrong choices are great: they cause trouble, and trouble is the story.
- **Flaws are handles.** A small flaw gives the reader something to hold and gives the story something to break. Perfect people are slippery. Nobody can hold on.
- **Give them one thing they are good at.** A flaw alone makes someone annoying. A skill makes us root for them.
- **Let the cost grow.** At first, failure costs a little. By the end, it should cost almost everything.

## Want, need, and cost at a glance

| Piece | Question it answers | Example |
|-------|--------------------|---------|
| Want | What is the hero chasing? | Win the science fair |
| Cost | What do they lose if they fail? | The scholarship, and Mom's trust |
| Flaw | What gets in their own way? | Never asks for help |
| Need | What must they learn? | That teamwork is not weakness |

## Three worked examples

### Example 1: The wish instead of the want

**Weak version:**
> "Jordan wants to be popular."

**Why it fails:** Popular how? With whom? What would Jordan *do*? A wish has no first step, so the reader cannot picture a scene. Also, there is no cost. If Jordan stays unpopular, life goes on.

**Stronger version:**
> Jordan wants to get a seat at the lunch table by the windows, because that is where Kai, her old best friend, sits now. If she cannot get over there before the winter dance, Kai will go with someone else and the friendship is probably over.

The want is a place and a person. There is a deadline. The cost is a friendship. Now you can imagine Jordan carrying her tray across the cafeteria, and your stomach knots up.

### Example 2: The hero who gets pushed around

**Weak version:**
> Sam is walking home. A stranger gives him a map. A storm comes. A boat appears. Sam gets in the boat. A pirate tells him what to do.

**Why it fails:** Things keep happening *to* Sam. He never decides anything. Readers lose interest when the hero is a leaf in the wind.

**Stronger version:**
> Sam finds the map in the storm and could throw it away, but he recognizes his missing brother's handwriting on the margin. He chooses to steal a boat, a decision that gets him in trouble before he even leaves the dock.

Same events, but now Sam makes a choice, and it costs him something. Choice is what turns a character into a hero.

### Example 3: The flaw with no price

**Weak version:**
> "Riley is stubborn. But it never causes problems."

**Why it fails:** A flaw that never matters is just a label. It is like putting a fire alarm on the wall that never rings.

**Stronger version:**
> Riley is stubborn. At the start, her stubbornness gets her the lead in the school play. Halfway through, it makes her ignore the director's warning, and the set collapses. At the end, she has to be stubborn in a new way: refusing to quit even after being laughed at.

The flaw shows up early, causes real damage in the middle, and gets turned around at the end. That is a flaw doing a job.

## How to build a hero in five lines

Fill in the blanks. Do it fast, then fix it later.

1. **Name and one detail:** "Marcus, who hums when he is nervous."
2. **Want:** "He wants to ___."
3. **Why now:** "It has to happen by ___ because ___."
4. **Cost:** "If he fails, he loses ___."
5. **Flaw:** "He gets in his own way by ___."

## Side characters need wants too

Your hero is not the only one with a destination. The best side characters want something of their own, and sometimes it clashes with the hero's want. A friend who wants to go home. A rival who wants the same trophy. A parent who wants the hero to quit. When two wants collide, you get scenes with built-in tension, and you barely have to work for it.

A quick check: pick any side character and finish the sentence "Right now, ___ wants ___." If you can't, they're furniture. Give them a want and watch them come alive.

## Percy Jackson check

Percy wants to clear his name and save his mom. He cannot walk away: his mom has been taken, and a war is coming. His flaw, a hot temper and a reckless streak, causes real problems and also saves the day. What he needs, to trust his friends and accept who he is, is what he finds along the way.

Look at how those pieces fit: a want he can say out loud (find the stolen bolt, get Mom back), a clock (ten days), and a cost that grows from "I get blamed" to "the gods go to war." He also makes choices. He chooses to go on the quest instead of hiding at camp. That is why he does not feel like a passenger.

A line in the same spirit, **our own** and not from the book: *"I had exactly two options: do the smart thing, or do the thing I was already doing."* It shows the reckless flaw in one breath.

## Common mistakes

- **The perfect hero.** No flaws means no friction. Give them something that costs them.
- **The vague want.** If you cannot film it, sharpen it.
- **No cost.** If nothing is at stake, the reader shrugs.
- **A flaw that is secretly a compliment.** "Her only weakness is that she cares too much" is not a flaw. Make it a real problem, such as lying, bragging, or running away.
- **A hero who only reacts.** Make them decide something big by the end of the first act.
- **A need that appears from nowhere.** Plant hints early, so the lesson feels earned.
- **Explaining the hero instead of showing them.** Do not write "Maya was brave." Write the scene where she walks into the dark hallway while her knees shake.
- **Changing the want halfway for no reason.** Wants can deepen, but they should not randomly swap.

## Mini-quiz

**1. What is the difference between a want and a need?**
*Answer:* The want is what the hero chases. The need is the lesson or truth they must learn. Stories often let them chase one and find the other.

**2. Why is "Riley is stubborn, but it never causes problems" a weak flaw?**
*Answer:* A flaw has to cost the hero something, or it does no work in the story.

**3. How can you tell if a want is specific enough?**
*Answer:* If you could film it as an action or a result, it is specific.

## Try it (20 min)

Fill in:

> "My hero wants ___ because ___. If they fail, they lose ___. Their flaw is ___."

If you cannot fill all four, the hero is not ready. Spend ten more minutes making each blank sharper. Then write one short scene (about 150 words) where the hero's flaw gets them into trouble on the way to the want.

## Level up

Do the **three-choice test**. Take your hero and list three big decisions they make in your story. For each one, write:

- What are the options?
- Which one does the hero pick, and why?
- What does it cost?

If a decision costs nothing, either raise the price or cut the scene. After that, try the **mirror scene**: write a short moment near the start where the hero faces a tiny version of their flaw, and another near the end where they face the same thing again but choose differently. A reader should feel the change without being told. The same small situation twice, with a new answer the second time, is one of the strongest tricks in all of storytelling.
