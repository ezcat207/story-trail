# The One-Sentence Story: Your Compass
*8 min read*

**The picture:** A compass does not show every tree. It shows which way is north. Your one-sentence story does the same. When you are lost in chapter six, it tells you which way the story is going. You can wander left and right, climb a hill, chase a squirrel. But you always know where north is.

Writers call this sentence a **logline**. (A logline is a one-line summary of a story, the kind you might read on the back of a movie case.) You do not need it for the reader. You need it for you.

## Why one sentence?

Because a story you cannot say in one breath is usually a story you do not understand yet. That is not an insult. It is a clue. If your explanation starts with "Well, it's complicated, there's this world, and it has three kingdoms, and..." then the story is still fog. Fog is fine early on. Fog is not fine when you have written 5,000 words.

## The formula

> When **[something happens]**, a **[kind of hero]** must **[goal]** before **[deadline or cost]**, but **[what stands in the way]**.

Not every story fits it exactly, but fill in the blanks and you will find out fast whether you have a story or just a cool setting.

## Test your sentence

- Is there a **person** with a **want**? (Not "a world with magic.")
- Is there something **blocking** the want?
- Is there a **cost** if they fail?
- Could a stranger repeat it back in one breath?
- Does it show **change**? (Something happens that pushes the hero off their normal path.)
- Is it specific? "A girl goes on an adventure" is true of ten thousand books. Add a detail that is only yours.

## Three worked examples

### Example 1: The setting-only sentence

**Weak version:**
> "A story about a floating city where people live in the clouds."

**Why it fails:** It describes a place, not a plot. There is no hero, no want, no problem. A reader cannot tell what will happen. You could write a thousand pages and never get to a story.

**Stronger version:**
> When the cable holding her floating city to the mountain starts to fray, a young rope-mender named Tali must find the one person who knows how to fix it before the city drifts out to sea, but the only expert is the man who was banished for cutting a cable years ago.

Now there is a person (Tali), a goal (fix the cable), a clock (before the city drifts), and an obstacle (the banished expert might be the villain). The cloud city is still there. It just got a job.

### Example 2: The vague goal

**Weak version:**
> "A boy who is sad about his dog dying has to learn to be happy again."

**Why it fails:** "Learn to be happy" is a wish, not a goal. You cannot see it. There is no clock and nothing to do. The story has no way to move from scene to scene.

**Stronger version:**
> When his late dog's tag turns up in a stranger's yard, a grieving eighth-grader must sneak into the neighbor's house before the family moves away on Saturday to find out whether the dog was really his, but the neighbor's teenage daughter has caught him and has her own reasons to keep him quiet.

The feeling (grief) is still the heart. But now it has an outside action to carry it. Readers feel sad things best when characters are doing something about them.

### Example 3: The too-crowded sentence

**Weak version:**
> "A teen wizard, a robot sidekick, and a talking cat must stop the evil queen, find the lost crown, escape the volcano, and win the school talent show before the moon turns red and also his parents get a divorce."

**Why it fails:** Too many goals. A compass cannot point to five norths. A reader cannot hold this in one breath, and neither can you.

**Stronger version:**
> When the moon begins to turn red, a clumsy teen wizard must find the lost crown before the queen does, but his only guide is a robot that keeps losing the map.

You can add the cat, the talent show, and everything else later as scenes in the story. The sentence keeps just the spine.

## How to write one fast

1. Write the messy version, as long as you like.
2. Cross out anything that is not the hero, the want, the clock, or the blocker.
3. Put it in the formula.
4. Read it out loud. If you stumble, simplify.
5. Check the four questions above.

## Fixing a sentence that feels flat

Sometimes you fill in every blank and the sentence still feels dead. Here are four quick repairs.

- **Sharpen the hero.** "A girl" is blank paper. "A girl who cannot stop telling the truth" is a person. One odd trait is enough.
- **Raise the cost.** Ask what the hero loses if she fails. Then ask what else she could lose. Pick the one that hurts the most.
- **Shrink the clock.** "Someday" is not a deadline. "By Saturday night" is. Short clocks make readers lean forward.
- **Make the blocker personal.** A storm is an obstacle. A storm plus your estranged brother steering the only boat is a story.

Try the repair that fits your sentence, then read it out loud again. Does your voice speed up? That's a good sign. If it slows down, you probably added a detail that does not carry weight, so cut it.

Also remember that a sentence can be fun. If you smile while writing it, readers will smile too. The best loglines have a tiny spark of irony, such as a hero who is the worst possible person for the job. A shy kid must lead the school tour for a famous visitor. A cat-sitter must guard a cat that is secretly a king. Contrast is cheap and powerful.

## Percy Jackson check

When a god's weapon is stolen and Percy is blamed, he must find it and return it within ten days before the gods start a war, but monsters and a traitor are in his way. One breath. Hero, want, clock, blockers.

Notice what that sentence leaves out: the summer camp, the Greek jokes, the road trip stops, the Oracle. All of those are in the book. None of them are in the compass. That is how a compass works. The sentence is the spine, and the story is the whole body.

Here is a sentence in the same shape, **our own** and not from the book: *"When the school mascot costume is stolen and Dani is blamed, she must track it down before Friday's big game, but the real thief is someone on her own team."* Same engine, different car.

## Common mistakes

- **Describing the world, not the plot.** If the sentence has no verb for the hero, it has no story.
- **Using a vague goal.** "Find herself," "be happy," and "grow up" cannot be seen. Make the goal something you could film.
- **Leaving out the clock.** No deadline means no pressure. The story can sit forever.
- **Skipping the blocker.** Without an obstacle, the hero just walks to the goal. Boring.
- **Listing every character.** Name the hero. Hint at the rest.
- **Spoiling the ending.** The sentence sets up the question. It does not answer it.
- **Never updating it.** If the story changes while you write, change the sentence. A compass that points the wrong way is worse than none.

## Mini-quiz

**1. What are the four things a good one-sentence story needs?**
*Answer:* A hero, a goal (want), a deadline or cost, and an obstacle.

**2. Why is "a boy must learn to be happy again" a weak goal?**
*Answer:* It is not something you can see or do. A goal needs an action the hero can take.

**3. Should the sentence include the ending?**
*Answer:* No. It sets up the problem and leaves the reader asking how it ends.

## Try it (20 min)

1. Pick your story idea from the last chapter.
2. Write your sentence three times, each with a different blocker. For example: a person, a place, and the hero's own flaw.
3. Pick the one that makes you want to know what happens.
4. Write it on a sticky note and put it where you will see it while you write.

## Level up

Try the **reverse logline**. Pick a book, movie, or game you love. Without looking it up, write its one-sentence story using the formula. Then check your sentence against a friend's version of the same story. Where did you disagree? Those gaps show what each of you thought the story was *about*.

Then try the **twist swap**: take your own sentence and change just one word in the "but" part. If the blocker was "a rival," try "a storm." If it was "a storm," try "a promise she made." Notice how a single swap sends the whole story down a different road. When you feel a little jealous of the new version, you have found a better compass.
