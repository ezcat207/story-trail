# Collecting Ideas: Fill Your Pantry
*8 min read*

**The picture:** A chef does not invent dinner from nothing at 6 p.m. They have a pantry. Flour, onions, a weird jar of pickled something. When it is time to cook, they look at the shelves and start combining. Your head needs a pantry too. Ideas come when you have stuff on the shelves.

Most people think writers wait for a big lightning bolt of an idea. Real writers mostly do something less glamorous. They collect small things all the time, and later they notice how the small things fit together.

## The rules

- **Carry a catcher.** Notes app, voice memos, a pocket notebook, the back of your hand if you must. An idea you do not catch is gone in about ninety seconds.
- **Collect three kinds of things:**
  - **Weird facts** ("a town in Norway where it is against the law to die").
  - **Real moments** (an argument you overheard, the way your uncle laughs before he tells a lie).
  - **"What if" questions** ("what if the school cafeteria menu was a secret code?").
- **Do not judge while collecting.** Judging is a different job for a different day. If you judge each idea the second it arrives, you will scare the good ones away.
- **Mix two unrelated things.** A lighthouse plus a pizza delivery. A ghost plus a group chat. The collision is where stories start.
- **Steal the feeling, not the plot.** If a movie made your stomach drop, ask what made it drop. Use that trick in your own world, with your own people.
- **Ask "and then what?" twice.** The first answer is usually the obvious one. The second is where it gets interesting.

## From a spark to a story idea

A spark is not yet a story. A **spark** is just a cool thing: an image, a fact, a feeling. A **story idea** is a spark plus a person who wants something and has trouble getting it. The trick is turning one into the other.

Here is a simple recipe:

1. Start with the spark.
2. Ask: *Who would care most about this?*
3. Ask: *What do they want because of it?*
4. Ask: *What could go wrong?*

## Three worked examples

### Example 1: A spark with no story

**Weak version:**
> "A haunted vending machine."

**Why it fails:** It is a cool image, but nothing is happening. No one wants anything. It is a poster, not a plot. Many beginners stop here and wonder why they cannot write page two.

**Stronger version:**
> Maya works the night shift at a bus station with her grandfather. Every night at 2:14 a.m., the vending machine gives her a snack she did not pay for, and each snack matches something that will go wrong tomorrow. A cracked cookie, a spilled juice. When it gives her a candle, she has to figure out who in her life is about to be in the dark.

Now there is a person (Maya), something to figure out, and a ticking feeling. The spark got a heartbeat.

### Example 2: Mixing two ideas

**Weak version:**
> Take "a dragon" and "a school." Result: a dragon attends a school. The end.

**Why it fails:** You put two things next to each other, but nothing crashes. A mash-up needs a **friction point**, which is the place where the two things rub badly against each other.

**Stronger version:**
> A dragon must pass the school's hall-pass rule: students may not leave class without a signed slip. But the dragon's wing is too big for the door and the teacher says, "Rules are rules." Now the dragon is trapped and tired, and a student named Eli has to decide whether to break a rule to help.

The friction is the rule meeting the wing. A funny problem becomes a real choice for Eli. That is a story.

### Example 3: Turning a real moment into a plot

**Weak version:**
> "I once got lost at the mall. I will write about that."

**Why it fails:** Real life is a great supplier, but a diary entry is not a story. Real life has no shape. It wanders and fizzles out.

**Stronger version:**
> Keep the feeling (the cold drop in your stomach when you cannot find your parent) and change everything else. Put a twelve-year-old girl in a mall that closes early because of a storm. The lights go off in sections. The only other person left is a security guard who is acting oddly. She wants to get out, and the doors are locking one by one.

You kept the true emotion and built a made-up engine around it. That is the best use of real life.

## Where to hunt for ideas

| Place | What to look for |
|-------|------------------|
| School hallways | Odd rules, rumors, who sits with who |
| Family dinners | Stories adults tell again and again |
| News headlines | A strange event that leaves you asking "why?" |
| Old photos | A face with a secret in it |
| Your own embarrassments | They are full of feeling |
| Dreams | Write them down right away, they fade fast |
| Songs | Pick one lyric and ask what happened before it |

## Keeping the pantry fresh

A pantry gets stale if you only ever add to it. Once a week, spend five minutes with your list. Cross out items that no longer excite you. Star the ones that still make you curious. Group similar items together, because patterns show up fast: maybe half your notes are about secrets, or about being the new kid. Patterns like that tell you what you care about, and caring is what makes a story feel alive.

Also feed the pantry on purpose. Read a book outside your usual taste. Watch a documentary about something you know nothing about. Ask a grandparent what the strangest job they ever had was. Strange input makes strange, fresh output.

## Percy Jackson check

The big idea of *The Lightning Thief* is a mix. Take ancient Greek gods. Drop them in modern New York. Make a kid with ADHD and dyslexia the hero, and explain those as signs of his demigod brain. (ADHD makes it hard to sit still and focus. Dyslexia makes reading words tricky. In the book, the restlessness is battle-ready instincts and the reading trouble is a brain wired for ancient Greek.)

Old myths plus a today-kid plus a clever twist equals a pantry mash-up. And look at the friction: a god-sized problem lands on a kid who is just trying not to get kicked out of school again. That tension gives the jokes and the stakes.

Here is a line in the same spirit, **our own** imitation and not from the book: *"My math teacher turned out to be a retired sea monster, which honestly explained the pop quizzes."* The joke works because the weird thing sits next to the boring thing.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting for the "perfect" idea.** It does not exist. Good ideas are usually just okay ideas that were developed well.
- **Judging too fast.** "That's dumb" is the quickest way to kill something that might have grown.
- **Stopping at the cool image.** An image without a person and a problem is a wallpaper.
- **Copying a plot beat for beat.** Borrowing a feeling is fine. Borrowing every event is just a worse copy.
- **Keeping ideas only in your head.** Your head is a leaky bucket. Write them down.
- **Choosing the first idea, always.** Make a list of at least ten. The first few are usually the ones everyone else would also think of.
- **Collecting forever and never writing.** A pantry is for cooking. At some point, light the stove.

## Mini-quiz

**1. What is the difference between a spark and a story idea?**
*Answer:* A spark is a cool image or fact. A story idea adds a person who wants something and has trouble getting it.

**2. Why does mixing two things work better when there is a "friction point"?**
*Answer:* Friction creates a problem, and problems create choices, which create plot.

**3. Why should you avoid judging ideas while collecting them?**
*Answer:* Judging early scares off ideas that might grow. Collect first, choose later.

## Try it (20 min)

1. Set a timer for 10 minutes.
2. Write 10 "what if" lines in a row without stopping. Do not cross anything out. Silly is fine.
3. Circle two that could mash together.
4. With the last 10 minutes, write one paragraph that combines the two. Ask: who wants what, and what is in the way?

Do not worry if it is messy. You are not building the house yet. You are finding the lumber.

## Level up

Start a **pantry file**. Make one note on your phone or page in a notebook called "Pantry." For the next seven days, add at least three items a day: one weird fact, one real moment, one "what if." On day seven, read the whole list and highlight everything that gives you a little shiver.

Then try the **rule of three collisions**. Pick any three items from your pantry and force them into a single story idea. A lighthouse, a group chat, and a missing shoe? Fine. Write a one-paragraph pitch. Some combos will be silly. One of them might surprise you, and that is the whole point. The more shelves you stock, the more meals you can make.
